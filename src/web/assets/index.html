Part 1

Hypothesis

Day-ahead trading in DK1 and DK2 is based on forecasts of wind and demand, which are never exact. The hypothesis is that these forecast errors move the price spreads between the day-ahead, intraday and balancing markets. Step 1 describes the forecast errors; step 2 relates them to the spreads.

Capacity calculation

Since 29 October 2024 the Nordic day-ahead market uses flow-based market coupling. The capacity calculation sets how much can be traded between bidding zones, and it rests on the TSOs' own forecasts.

  1. D-2
    Individual grid models (IGMs)

    Each TSO models its grid for every hour of the delivery day: topology, planned outages and its forecasts of load and generation, wind included. It also sends Nordic RCC its critical network elements and contingencies (CNECs) and its combined dynamic constraints, sets of lines whose summed flow is limited for stability, each with a maximum flow (Fmax).

  2. D-1, morning
    Common grid model (CGM) and PTDFs

    The TSOs update their models for new outages and forecast changes, which is common. Nordic RCC merges them into the common grid model and computes the power transfer distribution factors (PTDFs): how much a change in each zone's net position loads each element. HVDC links, and radial AC links to other synchronous areas, enter through advanced hybrid coupling (AHC): each gets a virtual bidding zone at its connection point with its own PTDFs, so its flow competes for grid capacity in the auction instead of having capacity reserved for it.

  3. D-1, morning
    Remaining available margins (RAMs)

    The RAM is the part of each element's capacity given to the market. F0 is the flow that would be on the element without any cross-zonal exchange, from the flows inside the bidding zones; it is taken from the grid model, linearised with the PTDFs, and is not available for trade. FAAC is the flow of capacity already allocated, FRA what remedial actions free up, and the flow reliability margin FRM is set from the spread of past differences between the flows expected in the capacity calculation and the realised flows. The TSOs validate the result. PTDFs and RAMs together form the flow-based domain.

  4. D-1, 11:00–12:00
    Day-ahead auction (SDAC)

    The flow-based parameters are published by 11:00. At gate closure at 12:00, the Single Day-Ahead Coupling clears prices and net positions inside the domain, with the Euphemia algorithm. It also respects allocation constraints: ramping limits and loss factors on the HVDC links.

  5. After the auction
    ATC extraction (ATCE) for intraday

    The flow already allocated in day-ahead is subtracted from each CNEC's margin, and an optimisation turns what is left of the domain into available transfer capacities (ATCs), border by border, as large as possible on all borders. These go to the Single Intraday Coupling (SIDC), both continuous trading and the intraday auctions (IDAs). Intraday capacities are not flow-based.

  6. Delivery
    Balancing (mFRR EAM)

    The cross-zonal capacity still free after intraday gate closure is left for the mFRR energy activation market.

  7. After delivery
    Imbalance settlement (eSett)

    Each balance responsible party pays or receives the imbalance price for the difference between its position and its metered production and consumption, per 15-minute period. Under the Nordic single price and single position (since November 2021), the price is the mFRR price in the dominating direction, or the day-ahead price without regulation, so an imbalance costs money when it adds to the system imbalance and can earn money when it reduces it. eSett settles on behalf of the TSOs.

Sources: Nordic RCC, nordic-rcc.net/flow-based, and the supporting document of the Nordic capacity calculation methodology; eSett, esett.com

TSO forecasts (used here)

  • Made by the TSOs and published on the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform: demand by 10:00 and wind at 18:00 on the day before delivery.
  • Closer to the inputs of the capacity calculation than to the forecasts market participants trade on.
  • A miss is built into the capacities everyone trades on, so it reaches all market participants at the same time. The miss is the TSOs'.

Market participants' forecasts (not used here)

  • Each participant bids with its own forecasts, from its own models or from consultants.
  • Their misses differ between participants and show up in each one's own imbalance.
  • They are not observed in this thesis.

The VARX model

A vector autoregression (VAR) models several variables together. Each one has its own equation with its own past and the past of the others, so a surprise in one variable can be followed through the others over the next hours.

The X stands for exogenous variables. They enter every equation in the same hour only and have no equation of their own. Here they are the wind ramp of the previous hour, the hydro situation, cross-border flows, and hour and month dummies. They act as controls, since the research question is about the endogenous variables. Only the endogenous variables have lags, so the model is a VARX(p, 0).

yt endogenous variables · xt exogenous variables · dt hour and month dummies · ut residuals · Ai, B, D coefficients, estimated by OLS.

Each model has the wind error, the demand error and one spread (K = 3). The three spreads cannot go in the same model, because DA-BAL = DA-ID + ID-BAL exactly. So each zone has three models.

Part 2 · VARX model

VARX tests

Before estimation, the series are tested for stationarity, the lag order is chosen and the residuals of the chosen model are checked.

Summary

Stationarity

A VARX needs series that move around a stable mean. A series with a unit root drifts away, and regressions on it can show relationships that are not there. Two tests are combined. ADF has a unit root as its null hypothesis, KPSS has stationarity. A series counts as stationary when ADF rejects and KPSS does not. When both reject, the result is a conflict.

The forecast errors give a conflict because their level moves over the year. The model already has hour and month dummies, so the tests are repeated on what is left after a regression on the same dummies. The usual critical values do not hold after this step, so they are simulated for each series.

Robustness of the stationarity tests

The decisions above depend on two settings. One is the KPSS bandwidth ℓ, the number of autocovariances that go into the long-run variance. A short bandwidth makes KPSS reject too often, a long one makes it weak. The baseline lets the data pick ℓ (). The other is the significance level α.

The checks repeat the tests with KPSS bandwidths of hours and at α = , on the series as they are and after the dummies. After the dummies, each bandwidth is compared with its own simulated critical value. With more than one year of data, the tests on the series as they are are also repeated on windows ending in the last year.

Test decisions

Lag selection

The lag order p is the number of past hours in each equation. Leaving out lags, such as the same hour the day before, biases the results; extra lags only cost precision. Information criteria weigh the fit against the number of coefficients, and the order with the lowest value is chosen. AIC, HQ and BIC differ only in the penalty.

All orders from 1 to 48 are scored on the same hours. The model uses the AIC order, because missing lags are the worse mistake. BIC and HQ pick fewer lags here, but move towards AIC as the sample grows.

Chosen lag orders

Residual checks

If the model captures the regular patterns, the residual of one hour says nothing about the residuals of the next hours. The LM test checks this for the first 24 lags. It rejects in every model, in its standard and in its robust form.

Dotted lines at one and two weeks.

The LM test

  1. Step 1
    Residuals

    The estimated VARX leaves three residuals in every hour, ût: one for the wind error, one for the demand error and one for the spread. They are the part of each variable that the model does not explain.

  2. Step 2
    Auxiliary regression

    Each residual is regressed on everything the model already uses, zt, and on the three residuals of each of the 24 previous hours. Dj holds the 3 × 3 = 9 coefficients of the residuals j hours earlier, so the test covers 24 × 9 = coefficients. Without a pattern they are all zero.

  3. Step 3
    Statistic

    gt multiplies the residuals of this hour with the past residuals qt, after removing what zt already explains of them. Without a pattern, positive and negative products cancel and their sum stays close to zero. The statistic divides the squared sum by how much the products vary from hour to hour. It works like a sum of squared t-values, one per coefficient: each says how far a coefficient is from zero, measured in its own chance variation.

  4. Step 4
    Critical value

    Without a pattern, each squared t-value behaves like the square of a standard normal number, so their sum follows a χ² distribution with degrees of freedom. Its average is and its standard deviation . The 5 % critical value, , is the value that only 5 % of samples without any pattern exceed. It lies standard deviations above the average. The p-value is the share of such samples that exceed the statistic found.

  5. Step 5
    Decision

    Above the test rejects no autocorrelation. The robust statistic is , standard deviations above the average, and p in every model.

Standard and robust form

The two forms differ only in how they measure the chance variation in step 3. The standard form takes it from one residual variance for the whole year: it compares the residual covariance with and without the past residuals, Σ̂v against Σ̂u. That is right only when every hour is equally volatile.

In 2025 calm and volatile weeks alternate. The ARCH-LM test finds a changing variance in equations, and the kurtosis is , against 3 for normal residuals (variance table below). In a volatile week the residuals of this hour and of the past hours are large together, so their products are large in both directions. The standard form expects them to be as small as in an average week, reads their size as a pattern and rejects too often, even when there is none. The robust form measures the variation of the products hour by hour, so a volatile hour raises the product and its expected variation together.

Here the standard statistic is times the robust one. Flipping the sign of each hour's residuals at random keeps the volatility of the data but removes any pattern. Over 200 such draws per model, the standard form still rejects in 80 to 96 % of them, the robust form in 5 to 10 %, close to the 5 % it should. In these draws the robust statistic exceeds 251 to 264 in 5 % of cases, close to the χ² critical value of 251. The robust form is used because its assumption fits the data. It rejects as well.

Why the test rejects

The test is very sensitive here. With hours and coefficients, small patterns add up: the robust statistic is times its average without a pattern.

The correlation chart above does not show the pattern, because the white-noise band, , is not the right yardstick for the first 24 lags. The model's 25 lags already absorb most of the correlation at these lags, so the residual correlations there sit closer to zero than those of a series that was never fitted, and their chance range is narrower than the band. A correlation inside the band, such as the largest within a day, , can still be significant. The test accounts for this through zt.

Split up, most of the evidence sits at lag 1 and in the wind-error equation. Lag 1 alone already rejects in every model. Tested equation by equation, the wind-error equation rejects in every model and the demand-error equation more weakly. The spread equation passes in the four balancing models (ID-BAL and DA-BAL) and rejects in the two DA-ID models. The lag-1 pattern of the wind error is strongest in the daytime hours, not at the change of day. Taken quarter by quarter, the test still rejects in most cases, so the pattern runs through the year and is not a single event.

More lags, up to 48, leave the statistic where it is, and the lags around one and two weeks lower it only a little. The weekly pattern below does not enter this test, which covers 24 lags.

What it means for the results

A pattern left in the residuals has two effects. The usual standard errors become too small, and since the model contains lagged values of its own variables, the coefficients can be biased. The first is handled: the Granger p-values and the response bands come from a bootstrap that keeps each two-week block of residuals together, so any pattern shorter than two weeks stays in the draws. The second is not measured. The Granger tests of the forecast errors on a spread sit in the spread equation, which shows no pattern in the balancing models. The responses over time also use the forecast-error equations, where most of the pattern is.

Options

  • Keep the model and report the rejection, as now. The bootstrap already keeps the pattern in the Granger p-values and the response bands.
  • Find the source of the lag-1 pattern in the wind-error equation, strongest in the daytime hours, and change the model there.
  • Check whether the pattern is non-linear or comes from links that change over the year. The generalized additive model and quantile regression of the next stage can show the first.

Weekly pattern

The main pattern left is weekly and sits in the demand-error equation. The demand forecast misses the same weekdays in the same direction for weeks at a time. Lags around one and two weeks remove it; hour-of-week dummies only reduce it.

Stability

A shock has to die out. This holds when every eigenvalue of the companion matrix is below 1 in absolute value.

Lagged wind ramp

The wind ramp rt and the wind forecast error et both contain actual wind production Wt, so in the same hour they are tied by an identity. With rt in the model, the wind-error equation just reproduced that identity, and its residuals kept a pattern no lag order removed. The ramp therefore enters as rt−1, which is known when the hour starts.

Details

Reading the results

Granger causality uses only the previous hours; the impulse response also includes the same hour.

Granger causality

Tests whether past forecast errors help predict the spread.

  • Reported as a p-value between 0 and 1.
  • A small p-value shows that the link exists, not how large it is.
  • Without any link, about 1 test in 20 is below 0.05 by chance.

Impulse response

How much the spread moves after a forecast error of typical size.

  • In EUR/MWh, for an error of one standard deviation.
  • The sign gives the direction; 0 means no effect.
  • If the 95 % band includes 0, the effect is not significant.

R²

Share of the hourly variation that each equation explains.

  • From 0 (nothing) to 1 (everything).
  • One dot per equation and model.

Method

Granger causality

x Granger-causes y if the past of x improves the prediction of y beyond y's own past and the rest of the model. In the VARX this is a test of whether all lags of x in the equation of y are zero. It is about prediction, not proof of cause and effect, and it leaves out effects within the same hour. Those show up in the impulse responses.

The test uses a Wald statistic with a covariance that allows the residual variance to change. The χ² p-value is not reliable here, so the p-value is bootstrapped. The model without the lags of x simulates new data, each two-week block of residuals gets a random sign, and the statistic is recomputed a thousand times.

Impulse responses

An impulse response follows the effect of one shock on all variables over the next hours. A stable VARX can be written as a sum of past residuals, so the effect h hours later comes straight from the coefficients.

The residuals are correlated within the hour, so they are first split into uncorrelated shocks with a Cholesky factorisation. This needs an order: the first variable can move the others within the hour, the last only itself. The order is wind error, demand error, spread, which assumes that prices cannot change the forecast errors of the same hour. This holds for wind, apart from curtailment at negative prices, but less for demand, since total load may include consumption that reacts to prices. The reference studies put the spread first, so its response in the hour of the shock is zero; the green lines show that order.

Forecast errors are forecast minus actual. A positive wind shock means less wind than forecast (a short system); a positive demand shock means less demand than forecast (a long system). Each shock is one standard deviation.
